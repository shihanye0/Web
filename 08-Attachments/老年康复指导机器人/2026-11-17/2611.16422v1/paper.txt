BlinkMimic: Training a Humanoid Motion Tracker
in Minutes on a Single GPU
Fuyi Chen
Abstract
Whole-body motion tracking lets a humanoid robot follow reference motions, but training a tracking
policy for a library of motions has usually taken hours of GPU time. SuperMimic showed that the policy
can instead be trained by backpropagating through a world model learned from parallel simulation, and
InstantTrack added Muon updates, a residual policy around the reference and an averaged deployed policy.
We build on both and present BlinkMimic, a recipe that makes this training faster. On a simulated Unitree
G1, BlinkMimic trains one policy for all 40 LAFAN1 clips in 3.4 minutes on a single RTX 4090, measured
until three consecutive checkpoints reach a normalized return of at least 0.82 with no failed clip (mean of
three seeds). We split training time into the cost of an iteration, the iterations needed to fit the motions, and
the iterations needed to remove the last failures, and assign each component, inherited or new, to one of
these terms. The new components are a step size that decreases once training has progressed, resets directed
by failures of the deployed policy under deployment conditions, and GPU graph capture of the whole
iteration. A leave-one-out ablation with three seeds per component shows which term each component
affects, and further measurements of the gradient, the update rule and the failure cases explain these effects.
Code and per-seed results are available at https://github.com/fuyichen0725/blinkmimic.
1 Introduction
A humanoid that can follow arbitrary reference motions is a building block for teleoperation, imitation and whole-body
skills (Peng et al., 2018; He et al., 2024; Liao et al., 2025). Training such a tracker has been expensive: with on-policy
reinforcement learning, a policy for a library of motions takes hours to days on several GPUs (Luo et al., 2023; Chen
et al., 2025). SuperMimic (Liao et al., 2026) took a different route. It learns a differentiable world model of the robot
from parallel simulation and improves the policy by backpropagation through time (BPTT) in that model, replacing the
high-variance policy-gradient estimate with a pathwise one. This cut the cost of training a tracker substantially and
made training time a quantity worth optimizing in its own right. InstantTrack (Dao, 2026) then showed in an open
implementation that a few changes, an averaged deployed policy, Muon updates (Jordan et al., 2024) and a policy that
outputs a correction to the reference, bring training on the 40 LAFAN1 clips (Harvey et al., 2020) down to minutes.
Training in minutes changes how such controllers are developed: a change to the reward, the retargeting or the robot
model can be tested within the same hour on one workstation.
This paper builds on both and asks how far, and why, this training can be made faster. Our recipe, BlinkMimic,
trains one policy for all 40 clips to a strict target, three consecutive checkpoints with a mean return of at least 0.82 and
no failed clip, in 3.4 minutes on one RTX 4090 (Figure 1). Recipes like this tend to accumulate changes without a clear
account of which ones matter. We organize ours around a simple identity: the time to the target is the cost of an iteration
times the number of iterations, and the iterations split into a fit phase, until the mean return reaches 0.82, and a tail
phase, until the last failing clips stop failing. The three terms are limited by different things. We assign each ingredient,
inherited from SuperMimic and InstantTrack or new, to one of them, and add new ingredients where a term was not yet
addressed.
The fit phase is limited by the update rule. The imagined gradient is accurate over the horizon on which the policy acts,
and regression onto a perfect teacher needs no fewer updates than learning from the imagined gradient. What remains is
how much each update achieves. Muon updates, here applied to both the policy and the world model, and the residual
policy shorten this phase, and we add a step size that starts high and falls once training has progressed.
The tail phase is limited by where the simulator is spent. The last failures occur at a few states that the deployed policy
reaches only after minutes of tracking. These states are learnable: the hardest clip trained alone stops failing early, but in
the mix the states before its hard spot receive about 1% of the practice, and failure statistics taken during training point
at the wrong places. A periodic probe with the deployed policy under deployment conditions finds these states directly.

The iteration is limited by the host. At these model sizes the GPU often waits for the CPU. We capture the whole
iteration, including the MuJoCo-Warp (Google DeepMind and NVIDIA, 2025) physics step and episode resets, as
CUDA graphs, and halve the imagination batch, which costs fewer extra iterations than it saves per iteration.
A leave-one-out ablation of the final recipe, with three seeds per ingredient, checks that each ingredient acts on the
term it was chosen for. Our contributions are:
• BlinkMimic, a single-GPU recipe that trains one policy for 40 LAFAN1 clips in 3.4 minutes, with code and per-seed
results (Table 1).
• A design organized by where training time goes, with a short analysis for each ingredient: how model error enters
the gradient, why the reward favors a residual policy, why the step size should fall, why normalized updates produce a
batch knee, and why failure statistics from training misdirect practice (Section 4).
• A leave-one-out ablation on a common baseline (Table 2) and measurements that explain it: gradient comparisons
between the model and the simulator, a perfect-teacher reference, batch forks with a prediction registered before the
run, and attribution tests for the tail (Section 5).
Section 3 describes the task and the training loop, Section 4 presents the ingredients term by term, and Section 5 reports
the result and the ablation and then examines the fit phase and the tail phase.
2 Related Work
Humanoid motion tracking. Physics-based tracking grew out of DeepMimic (Peng et al., 2018) and was scaled to large
libraries by PHC (Luo et al., 2023), MaskedMimic (Tessler et al., 2024), OmniH2O (He et al., 2024), GMT (Chen

et al., 2025) and BeyondMimic (Liao et al., 2025). These works use on-policy RL with massively parallel simulation.
SuperMimic (Liao et al., 2026) replaced the policy-gradient estimator with BPTT through a learned world model, and
InstantTrack (Dao, 2026) introduced EMA deployment, Muon and a reference residual in an open implementation. We
build on this line.
Differentiable simulation and model-based gradients. Pathwise gradients through simulators (Xu et al., 2022; Georgiev
et al., 2024) or learned models (Deisenroth and Rasmussen, 2011; Hafner et al., 2020, 2023) trade variance for bias,
which can be large at contacts and in chaotic rollouts (Suh et al., 2022; Metz et al., 2021); model error compounds over
the horizon (Janner et al., 2019; Buckman et al., 2018). Mixed estimators interpolate between the two gradient types
(Parmas et al., 2018; Suh et al., 2022).
Optimizers, batch size and step size. Muon (Jordan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025) orthogonalizes the update of each
weight matrix; SOAP (Vyas et al., 2024) preconditions in a Kronecker eigenbasis. The noise scale predicts a critical
batch for SGD-like training (McCandlish et al., 2018), and its interaction with momentum and normalization is subtle
(Zhang et al., 2019; Malladi et al., 2022; Cutkosky and Mehta, 2020). Adaptive methods sit near an edge of stability
(Cohen et al., 2022); range tests (Smith, 2017) and warmup–stable–decay schedules (Hu et al., 2024) set the step size in
practice.
Allocation and evaluation. Prioritized sweeping (Moore and Atkeson, 1993) and level replay (Jiang et al., 2021) spend
computation where learning progresses or fails, and imitation learning requires practice on the states the learner itself
reaches (Ross et al., 2011; Laskey et al., 2017). Time-to-target on stated hardware is the standard of DAWNBench
(Coleman et al., 2017) and MLPerf (Mattson et al., 2020); RL comparisons need multiple seeds (Agarwal et al., 2021).
3 Problem Setup
Task. A Unitree G1 with 29 actuated joints tracks reference clips in MuJoCo-Warp (Google DeepMind and NVIDIA,
2025) at 50 Hz. We use the 40 LAFAN1 clips retargeted to the G1 (441,140 frames, 2.45 hours; the average clip lasts 3.7
minutes). The policy outputs a normalized action that is scaled and passed as a joint position target to PD controllers.
The reward is a sum of Gaussian tracking kernels with an action-rate penalty, and an episode fails when the height or tilt
error of the anchor body exceeds a threshold, following SuperMimic (Liao et al., 2026).
Evaluation. The deployed policy runs deterministically, without domain randomization or noise, from the first frame to
the end of every clip, as in SuperMimic and InstantTrack. The normalized return ¯𝑅∈[0, 1] accumulates until the first
failure. A checkpoint meets the target when ¯𝑅≥0.82 with zero failed clips; Section 5 requires this at three consecutive
checkpoints.

4.1 Muon for the policy and the world model
InstantTrack (Dao, 2026) introduced Muon (Jordan et al., 2024) to this setting. Muon replaces the update of each hidden
weight matrix with 𝜂𝑈𝑉⊤, where 𝑈Σ𝑉⊤is the singular value decomposition of the momentum matrix. The step moves
every singular direction by the same amount; it is steepest descent under a limit on the spectral norm of the step. Input,
output and bias parameters keep Adam, and the learning rate is matched to Adam’s update magnitude (Liu et al., 2025).
Muon matters here because, as Section 5.3 shows, the fit phase is limited by how much each update achieves, not by
the quality of the imagined gradient: regressing the same network onto a perfect teacher takes 2.3× fewer updates with
Muon than with Adam. As in InstantTrack, the world model is also trained with Muon, which halves its loss early in
training.
4.2 A step size that starts high and falls on progress
A larger step removes the initial error faster but leaves more noise at the end. On a quadratic 𝐿= 1
2 ℎ𝑥2 with noisy
gradients of variance 𝜎2, the error contracts faster as the step 𝜂grows (up to 𝜂= 1/ℎ), while the remaining excess loss
is 𝜂𝜎2/(2(2 −𝜂ℎ)), which grows with 𝜂(Appendix A.6). Averaging the iterates, as our EMA policy does, lowers this
floor mainly along stiff directions. In world-model training the objective also moves between iterations, which adds to
the noise.
We therefore start at the largest policy step that a short screen admits, four times the base rate, and reduce it linearly
to the base rate over 300 iterations once the training return first exceeds 0.75, or at iteration 1400 at the latest. The
trigger adapts to how fast a seed learns, and the deadline covers seeds whose return rises late.

The model therefore uses the integrator’s structure, as does the implementation released with SuperMimic. It
predicts a velocity change and integrates position semi-implicitly, plus a small learned position correction; if it shared
the integration step exactly, its position Jacobian errors would be a factor Δ𝑡smaller than its velocity errors. Half of

the final recipe, where the model is also trained with Muon, removing the structure and the mirror no longer lengthens
the fit phase but lowers the return at which the policy settles, and with it the margin above the target (Section 5.2).
4.5 Spending the simulator on deployment failures
After the mean return reaches the target, a few clips still fail at a few places. Let 𝑝𝑖be the probability that the deployed
policy fails in 1-second bin 𝑖under deployment conditions, given that it got there. When failures are rare, the number of
failed clips is about Í
𝑖𝑝𝑖. If practice on a bin lowers its 𝑝𝑖, the fastest way to remove failures is to practise the bins with
the highest 𝑝𝑖first (Appendix A.7). Transfer between clips makes this only a guide, but it says what should drive the
reset distribution: failure rates of the deployed policy under deployment conditions.
Failure statistics collected during training measure something else. The training policy is noisy, randomized and not
averaged. If resets are weighted by those statistics, bins that cannot be learned under randomization keep absorbing
resets, while bins that fail only under deployment conditions receive no more than the uniform share (Appendix A.8).
BlinkMimic therefore measures deployment failures directly. Every 125 iterations from iteration 1000, a separate
simulator sweeps the whole library with the EMA policy under deployment conditions, using one world per 300-frame
tile started on the reference 200–450 frames before the tile. The first failure of each world is counted in its bin, counts
decay by 0.7 per sweep, and each count is spread over the failing bin and the 10 bins before it, because a failure develops
from the policy’s own drift. The resulting map receives 15% of all resets; the rest keep the sampler’s uniform, low-return
and training-failure terms (Appendix B). The probe needs no prior knowledge of which clips are hard.
4.6 Making each iteration cheap
Imagination batch. Because Muon fixes the length of the step, a smaller batch does not make the step noisier in size;
noise only rotates it away from the signal. A signal direction is lost once its strength falls below a detection threshold set
by the noise, as known for spiked random matrices (Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2012). The number of steps
to a target then stays nearly flat as the batch shrinks and rises sharply once the step’s overlap with the signal nears a
curvature cost (Appendix A.4), a knee rather than the smooth 1 + 𝐵noise/𝐵curve of SGD (McCandlish et al., 2018).
Since the position of the knee depends on the model, we measure it with short forks and take 𝐵= 2048, the smallest
batch that leaves the tail intact (Section 5.3).
Removing the host from the loop. At batch 2048, the updates and collection take 122 ms on an RTX 4090, but the
MuJoCo-Warp kernels of a collection step add up to only about 11 ms of its 31 ms; the rest is launch gaps and host
synchronizations. InstantTrack already captures the two update phases as CUDA graphs; we capture them from the first
iteration and add the collection step, with the policy inference, physics step and reward in one CUDA graph with episode
resets done by fixed-shape operations inside that graph. The probe sweep is also a graph and runs alongside training
over 20 iterations. None of this changes the learning algorithm: after a masked reset every simulator field matches the
original bit for bit, except joint velocities, which differ by at most 2.4 × 10−7 (Appendix C). In two alternating paired
runs on the same GPU, without checkpoints or probes, the collection graph lowers the steady-state iteration from 127.5
to 118.5 ms (−7%) and shortens the first 700 iterations by 18 s.

5 Experiments
Protocol. Every checkpoint is evaluated on all 40 clips from the first frame, with four replicas per clip, because the
simulator is not bitwise reproducible and a clip near its margin can flip between evaluations. A checkpoint is clean when
the mean return is at least 0.82 and all 160 rollouts survive. The target is three consecutive clean checkpoints, reported
at the first of them, as in InstantTrack (Dao, 2026); we also report the first checkpoint with return at least 0.82, which
marks the end of the fit phase. We checkpoint every 100 iterations (about 13 s). Training time includes compilation,
graph capture, checkpoints and probes and excludes the offline evaluation. Runs that report time have the RTX 4090 to
themselves, and baselines are re-run on the same GPU with the same evaluator and checkpoint interval. The ablation
runs on a different GPU type and compares iterations, each row against the full recipe run on that same GPU type.
Because the simulator is not bitwise reproducible across GPUs, the full recipe needs more iterations there than on the
RTX 4090 (1422 against 1306 to 0.82, and 2067 against 1533 for the three-checkpoint rule), so numbers are compared
only within a table. Seed counts are stated for every number; several diagnostics use one seed and are marked as such.
5.1 Main result
BlinkMimic reaches the target in 3.32, 3.28 and 3.53 minutes for seeds 0, 1 and 2 (Table 1). The first checkpoint at return
0.82 comes at 2.94 minutes on average, so the tail phase costs 26 seconds. One iteration costs 135 ms including probes
and checkpoints. At the target, the RMS position error of the 14 tracked bodies is 4.1 cm and the mean joint-angle
error 4.0°; by iteration 3500 the body error falls to 3.4 cm (Table 3). Meeting the target does not guarantee that later
checkpoints stay clean: in seed 1, fallAndGetUp2_subject2 fails in all four replicas at iteration 3100, long after the
step size has settled. We return to such late flips in Section 6; Appendix E lists the earlier recipes.

5.3 Why the fit phase is limited by the update rule
The ablation shows that the update rule (Muon, step size) and the residual policy set the length of the fit phase. This
section follows one line of argument: the imagined gradient is accurate where it matters, a perfect teacher is not faster,
so what remains is how much each update achieves.
The imagined gradient is accurate where the policy acts. We test the model against the simulator in two ways. Along the
actual training path (Figure 3a), the change of return predicted by the model matches the simulator, and at no checkpoint
is the model more optimistic about the next policy than about the current one (model return minus simulator return
0.0151 against 0.0157 at iteration 1000, about 0.6% of the return). Online training thus leaves no measurable model
exploitation at the scale of one update; it does appear when the model is frozen, where the return stops near 0.70 with 14
clips failing (one seed). A step backward along the path loses 0.04–0.10, while steps forward change the return by little
after iteration 1000. Random directions of equal norm change nothing. The optimizer moves along stiff directions and
makes its net progress over hundreds of iterations, as expected near an edge of stability (Cohen et al., 2022). The EMA
gains a further 0.012 over the raw policy.

gradient is thus accurate at the horizon where the policy can act, and the long horizon mostly delivers the value estimate.
A perfect teacher is not faster. To ask how much better the signal could be, we train the same network with the same
optimizer by regression onto the actions of a converged policy, with DAgger-style data collection, and define 𝜅as the
ratio of RL updates to teacher updates needed to reach 0.82. The teacher is a reference, not a bound. Figure 4a traces 𝜅
along the history of the recipe. The first configuration needs 30% more updates than regression; the structured world
model brings 𝜅to 1.04. Muon on the policy speeds up the teacher by 2.3× (3494 to 1478 updates, and SOAP matches it
at 1505) but RL by less, so 𝜅rises to 1.83. Muon on the world model and the reference residual then bring it back to
about 1.0. Before the step-size schedule, the recipe needed 1549 iterations at batch 4096, against 1478–1499 for the
teacher; with the schedule, the release recipe needs 1306 at batch 2048, fewer than the teacher. A better signal therefore
has little left to give, while a better update rule, as the teacher’s 2.3× gain from Muon shows, can still help.
The batch knee. In-batch noise is small. Cosines between the gradients of two disjoint halves of the batch lie mostly
in 0.62–0.87 under Muon, and the teacher gains only 3% from a batch of ×2 or ×4. Most singular directions of the
gradient that carry energy remain above the detection threshold at smaller batches (Figure 4b). A measurement of the
resolved signal count alone did not explain the knee, however, so we fit the single parameter 𝜌= 0.53 of the knee model
(Appendix A.4) to the fork at 𝐵= 1024. Before the run at 𝐵= 512 we registered a prediction of 2280–2630 iterations;
the run took 2301 (Figure 4c, one seed). A further fork that kept 4096 samples for the critic and used 1024 for the
policy kept about 84% of the slowdown (1.17×), so the knee comes mostly from the policy gradient and not from critic
data. At 𝐵= 2048 the wall time to 0.82 falls by 17% with every seed faster, and we stop there because smaller batches
increase late failures. In the release recipe, with its larger step, the cost of halving the batch is higher: 1422 against 1138
iterations to 0.82 (Table 2), a 25% increase, still smaller than the 29% saved per iteration (122 against 172 ms in Table 4).
Step size. A step-size screen shows the same trade-off. At iteration 500 the return rises from 0.606 at ×2 to 0.659 at ×4
and falls to 0.603 at ×8, without divergence (Figure 4d). By iteration 1250, ×2 has caught up, consistent with the higher
noise floor of a large step (Section 4.2). In an earlier recipe, a progress-triggered decay from ×4 reduced iterations
by 19% against a constant step (three seeds; 14% in the final recipe, Table 2), whereas a cosine schedule with a fixed
horizon gave no gain.

6 Discussion and Limitations
What limits the recipe now. The fit phase takes more than 80% of the 3.4 minutes. It already runs at about the
perfect-teacher rate, and the ablation shows that every fit-phase ingredient still matters, so further gains there must come
from a better update rule, from less for the network to learn from scratch, or from cheaper updates. The clearest open
problem is reliability. A checkpoint that is clean at one time can fail later (Section 5.1), and the ablation suggests that
the margin above the target, which the world-model structure raises, matters for this alongside the allocation of practice.
We regard late flips, not mean speed, as the most valuable target for future work.
Scope of the evidence. We use one robot and one motion library, report training time on one GPU type, and do not
report real-robot deployment. Three seeds cannot resolve differences below about 15% in the strict rule, so we give
every seed. In the final recipe this applies to the allocation: it halves late failures, as it reduced them in an earlier recipe,
but the remaining counts are too small for three seeds to separate. The ablation keeps the settings tuned for the full
recipe, so a removed ingredient may do better with settings of its own, and the structure and the mirror are removed
together. Several diagnostics rest on one seed and were measured on earlier configurations of the recipe, as marked. The

asynchronous probe assumes an otherwise idle GPU: when another process shares the GPU, training with it can crash,
which the synchronous probe avoids at a cost in speed. The perfect-teacher ratio 𝜅is a reference and not a lower bound,
since a different update rule changes both numerator and denominator. The analyses in Section 4 are idealized: they say
what to measure and how to read it, and the design choices themselves rest on measurements.
Conclusion. BlinkMimic trains one policy for 40 LAFAN1 clips in 3.4 minutes on a single RTX 4090. Splitting the
time into iteration cost, fit and tail shows what to work on: the update rule during the fit phase, the allocation of the real
simulator during the tail, and the removal of host latency throughout. The split itself does not depend on the robot
or the motion library, so the same accounting can be applied to other world-model training setups; whether the same
ingredients matter there is a question for measurement, not something our single benchmark settles. We release the
recipe and per-seed results to make such comparisons easy.

C Systems Details
Time in an iteration. Before the collection graph, an iteration at batch 2048 took 122 ms: policy and critic updates
70 ms, world-model updates 21 ms, and collection 31 ms. The GPU was fully idle for only 8–11% of the iteration, so
overlapping simulation with learning on separate streams would gain little. Removing launch gaps gains more. Warp’s
own step graph cannot be launched inside a PyTorch capture, so we capture Warp’s kernels directly into the PyTorch
graph on a shared stream, and the constraint solver’s iteration loop becomes a conditional graph node. Episode ends are
handled with fixed shapes: random reset frames are drawn for every robot and written only where the episode ended,
which removes data-dependent indexing and host synchronizations.
Check of the reset path. We compare all simulator state fields after a masked reset with those after the original reset.
They agree bit for bit except for joint velocities, which differ by at most 2.4 × 10−7.

to the first checkpoint at return 0.82 and 26 s of tail.
